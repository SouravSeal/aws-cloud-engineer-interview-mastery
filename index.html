01 — Interview Principles

Strong candidates don't just know AWS services. They demonstrate structured reasoning, operational awareness and the ability to make defensible engineering decisions.

1. ClarifyRequirements, constraints, scale, users and success criteria.
2. DecomposeApplication, network, identity, data, platform and operations.
3. HypothesizeLikely causes or viable designs before choosing a service.
4. ValidateMetrics, logs, configuration and controlled tests.
5. RecommendSolution, trade-offs, risks and operational consequences.
Golden rule: Don't lead with an AWS service. Lead with the problem and decision criteria, then select the service that best satisfies them.

Think in failure domains

Ask what happens when an instance, AZ, dependency, network path, region or external service fails.

Make trade-offs explicit

Strong answers explain why a design is preferred and what it costs in complexity, latency, availability or money.

Separate mitigation from root cause

A production workaround may restore service. A good engineer also explains how the underlying failure will be prevented.

MODULE 01 • DEEP DIVE

Cloud & AWS Foundations

The foundation module is designed to make you interview-ready on the concepts that sit underneath almost every AWS question. The goal is not to memorize definitions; it is to understand the engineering implications.

Learning Outcomes

  • Explain cloud concepts using engineering language.
  • Reason about Regions, Availability Zones and failure domains.
  • Explain AWS shared responsibility by service model.
  • Use Well-Architected principles to evaluate designs.
  • Distinguish scalability, elasticity, availability and resilience.

Interview Focus

  • Why a design choice is appropriate.
  • What happens when a dependency fails.
  • Where responsibility sits between AWS and the customer.
  • How requirements change architecture decisions.
  • How to communicate trade-offs clearly.

Core Vocabulary

Region • Availability Zone • Fault domain • Elasticity • Scalability • Availability • Resilience • Managed service • Shared responsibility • Well-Architected

Interview principle: When asked a definition, give the definition, then immediately connect it to why it matters in production. Example: don't stop at “an Availability Zone is a separate location”; explain how AZ separation reduces correlated failure risk and enables highly available architectures.

FOUNDATIONQ01 • Cloud Fundamentals

What problem does cloud computing solve compared with traditional on-premises infrastructure?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: Cloud changes the economics and operating model of infrastructure. Instead of purchasing capacity upfront and operating physical infrastructure yourself, you can provision resources on demand, scale them, use managed services, automate operations and pay according to usage. The real benefit is not “someone else's servers”; it is faster access to capacity and higher-level infrastructure capabilities with less undifferentiated operational work.

Interviewer follow-up: When would on-premises still be a better choice? Discuss latency, regulatory constraints, existing investments, specialized hardware, data sovereignty and workload economics rather than saying cloud is always better.
FOUNDATIONQ02 • Global Infrastructure

What is an AWS Region and what is an Availability Zone?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: A Region is a geographic area containing AWS infrastructure. An Availability Zone is an isolated location within a Region designed to provide independent failure boundaries. Engineers use multiple AZs to reduce the impact of localized infrastructure failures while using multiple Regions when requirements justify broader geographic isolation, disaster recovery or data-residency considerations.

Do not say: “An AZ is just another data center.” AWS describes an AZ as one or more discrete data centers with redundant power, networking and connectivity.
FOUNDATIONQ03 • Region Selection

How would you choose an AWS Region for a production workload?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Decision framework: 1) required AWS services/features, 2) user/network latency, 3) regulatory and data-residency requirements, 4) availability and disaster-recovery strategy, 5) pricing and capacity considerations, 6) operational constraints and dependencies. A strong candidate says Region selection is a business and architecture decision, not simply “pick the closest Region.”
INTERMEDIATEQ04 • Architecture Vocabulary

Explain the difference between scalability, elasticity, availability and resilience.

Reveal interview-grade approach
Scalability: ability to handle increased or decreased workload by adding/removing capacity. Elasticity: ability to dynamically adjust capacity with changing demand. Availability: ability to remain accessible and operational when expected. Resilience: ability to withstand and recover from failures while continuing or restoring intended operation.

Interview insight: A system can be scalable without being highly available. It can also be highly available but poorly elastic. Strong answers separate these properties.
FOUNDATIONQ05 • Security Model

Explain the AWS Shared Responsibility Model.

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: AWS is responsible for security of the cloud — the underlying infrastructure AWS operates. The customer is responsible for security in the cloud, with the exact boundary varying by service. For example, with more abstracted managed services AWS operates more of the infrastructure stack, while customers still manage data, access controls and service configuration.

Key principle: Never describe shared responsibility as a fixed checklist independent of the service. Customer responsibility changes with the service model and configuration.
INTERMEDIATEQ06 • Service Model

How does the Shared Responsibility Model differ between EC2, RDS and S3?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: With EC2, the customer has substantial responsibility for the guest OS, patches, applications and security configuration. With RDS, AWS manages much more of the underlying database platform, while the customer remains responsible for items such as data, access, configuration and workload-level security. With S3, AWS manages the infrastructure and platform; customers still control data, access policies, encryption choices and appropriate configuration. The key is to explain the shift in operational responsibility rather than memorizing a table.
FOUNDATIONQ07 • Well-Architected

What are the six pillars of the AWS Well-Architected Framework?

Reveal interview-grade approach
The six pillars: Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Cost Optimization and Sustainability. In an interview, don't only list them. Explain that they provide a consistent way to evaluate workload design and make trade-offs based on business requirements. AWS notes that security and operational excellence are generally not treated as simple trade-offs against the other pillars.
INTERMEDIATEQ08 • Trade-offs

Can you optimize for all six Well-Architected pillars at the same time?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: You aim to improve across the pillars, but engineering decisions involve trade-offs. For example, multi-Region active-active architecture may improve resilience but increase cost and operational complexity. A good answer starts with business requirements and risk tolerance, then explains the trade-off rather than declaring one pillar “most important” in every workload.
FOUNDATIONQ09 • Resilience

What is a failure domain, and why does it matter?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: A failure domain is a boundary within which a failure can affect multiple components. Good architecture avoids placing all critical capacity inside the same failure domain. In AWS, AZ-aware design is a common example: distributing components across AZs reduces correlated impact from a localized infrastructure failure.
INTERMEDIATEQ10 • Managed Services

Why would you choose a managed AWS service instead of running the technology yourself?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: To reduce undifferentiated operational work and shift more responsibility to AWS. Evaluate patching, backups, scaling, availability, monitoring, operational expertise and integration requirements. But managed does not mean “zero operations”; customers still own configuration, access, data, application behavior and workload-level reliability. Also consider feature limitations, portability and cost.
SCENARIOQ11 • High Availability

Design a highly available application within one AWS Region.

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: Start with the availability target. Place application capacity across multiple AZs, use a load-balancing layer with health checks, avoid single-instance dependencies, design the data tier for the required failure mode, and make deployments/operations repeatable. Then ask: what happens if one AZ fails? The strongest answer walks through the failure path rather than stopping at “use multiple AZs.”
SCENARIOQ12 • Multi-Region

When would you consider a multi-Region architecture?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: When business requirements justify protection from Region-level disruption, geographic latency needs, regulatory constraints or global user distribution. Start with RTO/RPO and business impact, then compare backup/restore, pilot-light, warm standby or active-active approaches. Multi-Region should not be treated as a default “more highly available” answer because it introduces replication, consistency, routing, operational and cost complexity.
FOUNDATIONQ13 • Scalability

What is the difference between vertical and horizontal scaling?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Vertical scaling increases the capacity of an existing resource, such as moving to a larger instance. Horizontal scaling adds more instances/resources. In cloud architectures, horizontal scaling often supports resilience and elasticity better, but it may require stateless application design, distributed coordination and data-layer considerations.
INTERMEDIATEQ14 • Application Design

Why is statelessness useful when designing scalable cloud applications?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: If request-serving instances do not depend on local session state, traffic can be distributed across instances and instances can be replaced or scaled independently. State can be externalized to an appropriate durable or shared data store. But don't claim every application must be completely stateless; explain where state belongs and the consistency/latency trade-offs.
SCENARIOQ15 • Elasticity

Your traffic is unpredictable and spikes 10× during certain events. What would you investigate?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong approach: identify the traffic pattern and latency SLO, then examine horizontal scaling, load balancing, asynchronous buffering, caching, database scaling, rate limits and capacity limits. Consider whether scaling signals react quickly enough and whether downstream dependencies can absorb the spike. A strong answer treats the system as a chain: scaling the frontend alone is not enough if the database is the bottleneck.
FOUNDATIONQ16 • Decoupling

What does it mean to decouple components in a cloud architecture?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: Reduce direct dependency between components so they can evolve, scale and fail more independently. Messaging and event-driven patterns can buffer workload, smooth spikes and isolate failures. But decoupling introduces eventual consistency, retries, duplicate processing and operational complexity, so the trade-off must be explicit.
SCENARIOQ17 • Failure Handling

A downstream service becomes slow rather than completely unavailable. How should a cloud application respond?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: Protect the system with bounded timeouts, appropriate retry policies with backoff and jitter, idempotency where needed, concurrency limits, circuit-breaking or load shedding where appropriate, and graceful degradation. Monitor dependency latency and error rates. Avoid infinite retries: retries can amplify an incident and create a retry storm.
TROUBLESHOOTINGQ18 • Incident Thinking

Your application suddenly fails even though no deployment occurred. What is your first step?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: Establish scope and timeline before changing anything: which users, which Region/AZ, which API paths, what changed in metrics/logs, and whether an upstream dependency or AWS service is impaired. Check application and infrastructure telemetry, recent configuration changes, dependency health and relevant AWS service health information. The first objective is to localize the failure domain.
INTERMEDIATEQ19 • Observability

What does good observability mean for a cloud workload?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: It means having enough telemetry to understand system behavior and investigate failures: metrics for trends and saturation, logs for detailed events, traces for request paths across distributed components, plus meaningful alerts and dashboards. Strong observability connects technical signals to user impact and SLOs rather than collecting every possible log.
INTERMEDIATEQ20 • Automation

Why is Infrastructure as Code important in cloud engineering?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: IaC makes infrastructure repeatable, reviewable, testable and easier to recover. It supports version control, standardization, drift detection and automated delivery. But IaC does not automatically make infrastructure safe; you still need secure workflows, state protection, change review, testing and appropriate access controls.
FOUNDATIONQ21 • Cost

What are the first principles of cloud cost optimization?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: Know what you are spending and why, eliminate waste, right-size resources, align capacity with demand, choose appropriate pricing models, optimize storage/data transfer and continuously review utilization. Cost optimization is not simply “use the cheapest service”; it is achieving the required business outcome at an appropriate total cost.
ARCHITECTUREQ22 • Design Review

You are asked to review an AWS architecture. What framework would you use?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: Begin with business and workload requirements, then evaluate the design through the six Well-Architected pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization and sustainability. Identify high-risk issues, prioritize by business impact and propose measurable improvements. Avoid turning the review into a service-by-service checklist.
ARCHITECTUREQ23 • Resilience Review

How do you identify single points of failure in an architecture?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong approach: Trace the critical user journey end-to-end and ask what happens if each dependency disappears, becomes slow, loses network connectivity or returns incorrect data. Look for single instances, single AZs, single network paths, single control-plane dependencies, unreplicated data and shared operational components. Then determine whether each single point is actually acceptable given the business RTO/RPO and availability target.
ARCHITECTUREQ24 • Architecture Thinking

What questions should you ask before designing any AWS solution?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong discovery questions: What business problem are we solving? Who are the users? What is the workload pattern? Availability target? RTO/RPO? Data classification? Compliance? Latency? Throughput? Expected growth? Integration dependencies? Deployment frequency? Operational ownership? Budget? Required AWS Regions? Migration constraints? These questions prevent premature service selection.
SCENARIOQ25 • Meta Interview Skill

The interviewer challenges your design choice and proposes a different AWS service. How should you respond?

Reveal interview-grade approach
Strong answer: Don't defend the original design emotionally. Re-state the decision criteria, compare both options against those criteria and acknowledge where the alternative is better. Then explain which trade-off matters most for the stated requirements. This demonstrates engineering judgment and adaptability — both are stronger signals than trying to “win” the discussion.
